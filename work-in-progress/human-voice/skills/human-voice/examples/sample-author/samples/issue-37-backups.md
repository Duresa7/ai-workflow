A thing I keep getting wrong about backups, and the one rule that finally stuck.

I have lost data three times in my life. Once to a dead drive in 2009 (no backup, obviously). Once to a backup that had been silently failing for eleven months, which I found out about on the day I needed it. And once, embarrassingly, last spring, to my own script, which I'd written to "clean up old snapshots" and which had an off-by-one error that cleaned up the new ones instead.

Three losses, three different causes, and I notice now that none of them were the cause I'd been guarding against. I was always ready for the last disaster.

So here is the rule, and it's boring: a backup you have not restored from is a hypothesis. That's it. Not "test your backups," which everyone says and nobody does, but a specific reframe that changed what I actually do. Every quarter I pick one machine, pretend it's gone, and rebuild it from the backup onto a spare disk. Takes an afternoon. I've done it five times now and found a problem on two of them, which is a worse hit rate than I expected and exactly why I keep doing it.

The spare disk cost forty dollars. The afternoon costs more than that, honestly, but less than eleven months of false confidence did.
